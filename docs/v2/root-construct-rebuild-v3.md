# v3 root-construct and commitment measurement contract

Status: **frozen implementation authority for issue #20**. This is a political-theory/content-validity specification, not an empirical validation claim. Empirical validity remains `NOT_EVALUATED`.

## Method

The sorter treats ideologies as structured configurations of commitments rather than points in a universal ideological geometry. The implementation follows a morphological approach: constitutive/core/characteristic commitments can distinguish an ideology, contested commitments do not count against membership, and incompatible commitments can block a strong match. Taxonomic status, relationship type, abstraction level, and sorter implementation role remain separate.

The three measurement layers remain analytically separate:

- **Normative**: what values, rights, distributions, institutions, and social conditions ought to exist.
- **Descriptive**: how society, markets, states, power, class, culture, collective action, and historical change are understood to work.
- **Prescriptive**: what policies, institutions, reforms, and strategies should be used.

## Item rule

Every active scored item has exactly one **primary root construct**. A module-local specialist mapping may coexist only with an explicit audit rationale. Root cross-loadings are prohibited by default. An item is not allowed to load on another root construct merely because its answer is stereotypically associated with an ideology.

Every active item or statement-choice option receives a versioned audit record containing: layer, primary construct, polarity/response interpretation, construct rationale, source scope, boundary note, removed legacy mappings, and review disposition.

Specific-study knowledge claims are not treated as general descriptive worldview measures unless the item itself asks for a general mechanism or expectation.

## Normative root constructs

These are unipolar commitments: the positive pole is endorsement of the named commitment; a negative score is rejection, not automatic endorsement of a different concept.

1. `liberty-noninterference` — freedom from coercive or intentional interference.
2. `effective-agency` — freedom as practical power/capability to pursue ends.
3. `anti-domination` — freedom from uncontrolled/arbitrary power; not generic dislike of hierarchy.
4. `political-authority-legitimacy` — moral legitimacy of coercive political authority and obligation.
5. `legal-equality` — equal legal/civic status and nondiscrimination.
6. `equality-opportunity` — fair access to positions/opportunities.
7. `relational-equality` — opposition to social relations of domination, status hierarchy, and second-class standing.
8. `distributive-equality` — reducing unjust material/economic inequalities as an end of justice.
9. `productive-property` — strong private ownership/control claims over productive capital.
10. `workplace-self-government` — workers’ normative claim to meaningful governance/control of productive institutions.
11. `land-natural-resource-claims` — private versus common/social claims over land and natural-resource rents.
12. `personal-property` — strong claims over personal possessions and ordinary-use property.
13. `intellectual-property-rights` — normative legitimacy of exclusion rights over inventions, expression, information, and related intangible creations.
14. `universal-moral-standing` — basic moral standing is not reduced by nationality or political borders.
15. `compatriot-priority` — special duties to compatriots may legitimately receive political priority.
16. `national-self-determination` — nations may have claims to collective political self-government.
17. `ecological-standing` — ecological systems/nonhuman nature possess political or moral standing beyond instrumental human use.
18. `intergenerational-ecological-duty` — present generations owe duties concerning ecological conditions inherited by future people.
19. `tradition-deference` — inherited institutions/practices deserve presumptive weight.
20. `coercive-moral-order` — law may legitimately enforce a substantive traditional moral order in private/social life.
21. `religious-establishment` — public institutions may constitutionally privilege or establish a religion.
22. `religious-neutrality` — the state should remain institutionally neutral among religion and non-religion.
23. `religious-accommodation` — law should sometimes accommodate conscience/religious exercise rather than require identical treatment.
24. `religious-political-inspiration` — religious moral traditions may legitimately inspire ordinary democratic political projects without establishing clerical authority.
25. `force-permissibility` — military/organized force can be morally justified under at least some conditions.
26. `anti-militarism` — military power, prestige, or intervention should be politically constrained beyond ordinary just-war limits.
27. `democratic-legitimacy` — collective political power normally requires democratic authorization/accountability.
28. `social-solidarity` — political community has obligations to secure members against severe social/material vulnerability.

## Descriptive root constructs

1. `market-knowledge-coordination` — prices/exchange can aggregate dispersed information and coordinate resources.
2. `market-power-concentration` — market institutions can generate durable private concentration/monopoly/dependency.
3. `state-administrative-capacity` — states can competently implement complex policies.
4. `political-incentives-capture` — public institutions are vulnerable to organized interests, rent seeking, and insider incentives.
5. `democratic-decision-capacity` — democratic processes can aggregate information/preferences and correct errors.
6. `expert-knowledge-capacity` — specialized expertise can improve collective decisions under suitable institutions.
7. `cultural-malleability` — norms/identities/cultural practices respond substantially to institutional and policy change.
8. `decentralized-coordination-capacity` — noncentralized voluntary/federated institutions can solve coordination problems.
9. `class-exploitation` — class relations and control of productive assets systematically generate exploitation or asymmetric workplace power.
10. `capital-accumulation-crisis` — capitalist accumulation tends toward concentration, instability, or recurrent crisis mechanisms.
11. `ideology-hegemony` — beliefs/cultural institutions systematically reproduce or legitimate structures of power.
12. `nation-formation` — national identities are socially/institutionally produced and historically reproduced rather than simply given.
13. `patriarchy-social-reproduction` — gendered power and social reproduction systematically structure institutions and opportunities.
14. `imperialism-dependency` — international political-economic relations can reproduce domination/dependency across states and peoples.
15. `ecological-limits-growth` — biophysical constraints can limit or alter the feasibility of indefinite material throughput/growth.
16. `collective-action-capacity` — groups can overcome free-rider/coordination problems under identifiable institutional conditions.
17. `institutional-path-dependence` — inherited institutions constrain later feasible choices and reform trajectories.
18. `historical-change-structural` — large-scale political change is substantially shaped by structural social/economic forces, not only individual preferences or leaders.

## Primary ideology boundary matrix

The production primary roster is the 12 commitment-backed outcomes already retained after #18. The following discriminants must have direct active measurement or the relevant comparison abstains:

- Christian Democracy ↔ Prudential Conservatism: `social-solidarity`, `subsidiarity`, `religious-political-inspiration`.
- Classical Liberalism ↔ Right-Libertarianism: strength of `productive-property`/`liberty-noninterference` plus treatment of `effective-agency`, `social-solidarity`, and public provision as contested rather than constitutive.
- Democratic Socialism ↔ Social Democracy: `public-ownership` and/or `worker-control` versus regulated-capitalist `social-insurance`/`income-redistribution`.
- Libertarian Socialism ↔ Democratic Socialism: `political-authority-legitimacy`, `institutional-decentralization`, and `exit-parallel-institutions`/direct organization.
- Marxian Socialism ↔ Democratic Socialism: direct descriptive commitments to `class-exploitation`, `capital-accumulation-crisis`, `ideology-hegemony`, and structural historical change.
- Marxist-Leninism ↔ Marxian Socialism: `vanguard-party`, `democratic-centralism`, and `central-planning`/state-directed transition.
- Republicanism ↔ Social Liberalism/Classical Liberalism: constitutive `anti-domination` plus `contestable-institutions`, not merely generic civil liberty.
- Social Liberalism ↔ Classical Liberalism: stronger `effective-agency`, `social-solidarity`, and legitimate social provision while retaining liberal rights.
- Green Politics ↔ neighboring left/liberal outcomes: constitutive `ecological-standing` plus intergenerational/ecological-restructuring commitments.

## Source basis

The implementation should add explicit provenance records for at least the following research authorities:

- Michael Freeden, *Ideologies and Political Theory: A Conceptual Approach* (Oxford University Press), for morphological ideology analysis.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Liberalism” and “Positive and Negative Liberty,” for non-interference, effective freedom/autonomy, and republican non-domination as distinct concepts.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Equality” and “Equality of Opportunity,” for legal/political, relational, distributive, and opportunity equality distinctions.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Property and Ownership,” and “Intellectual Property,” for distinct objects/forms of property and IP-specific justification.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Political Legitimacy,” “Authority,” and “Political Obligation,” for authority/obligation distinctions.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Nationalism,” plus the Oxford Handbook treatment of nationalism, for universal standing, compatriot partiality, national identity, and self-determination as separable commitments.
- Oxford Handbook treatments of Conservatism, Social Democracy, Green Ideology, Marxism/Marxian tradition, Communism, Republicanism, and Libertarianism.
- Cambridge University Press work on Christian democracy (personalism, subsidiarity, social capitalism, Christian inspiration).
- Oxford *Religion in Liberal Political Philosophy* and the Oxford Research Encyclopedia entry on political secularism for non-establishment, neutrality, accommodation, conscience, and religious inspiration as separate questions.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Pacifism,” for the continuum from absolute nonviolence to anti-war positions and its distinction from conditional force permissibility.

Those remain separate empirical gates.
