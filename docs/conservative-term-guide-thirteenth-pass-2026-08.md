# Conservative term-guide thirteenth pass

Date: 2026-08-11

## Scope

This pass reviewed eight conservative or liberal-conservative labels whose interface guides fell back to generic Conservatism or Liberalism definitions. It adds direct term guides, layer-specific summaries, and narrow metadata corrections. It does not change centroids, axes, questions, weights, label roles, result eligibility, or scoring behavior.

## Direct term guides

- **Neoconservatism** now identifies the modern U.S. current rooted partly in Cold War liberal and anti-communist circles and later associated with assertive internationalism, democracy promotion, and willingness to use military force. It is not defined as generic hawkishness or as Straussian philosophy.
- **Paleoconservatism** now identifies the U.S. movement named in the 1980s to revive parts of the Old Right against neoconservative and neoliberal currents. It is not presented as a generic name for all old or pre-Enlightenment conservatism.
- **One-Nation Conservatism** now identifies the British paternalist tradition that accepted a cost-conscious, non-egalitarian welfare state and moderate intervention for cohesion, security, and opportunity.
- **Fiscal Conservatism** now identifies a thin concern with deficits, debt, and sustainable public finances rather than a complete conservative worldview or one mandatory tax-and-spending formula.
- **Social Conservatism** now identifies the preservation of inherited moral and social institutions without assigning one economic or foreign-policy program to every social conservative.
- **National Conservatism** now identifies priority for national sovereignty, cultural continuity, and the nation-state while acknowledging substantial disagreement over economics, welfare, democracy, and external power.
- **Conservative Liberalism** and **Liberal Conservatism** now state the catalog’s intended difference: the former is placed in the liberal family and the latter in the conservative family. Both guides warn that scholarship and party usage do not impose a universal word-order taxonomy.

Each guide is keyed to its stable catalog id and shown alone, so a specific result is no longer displaced by a generic parent-family definition.

## Metadata and layer corrections

- Neoconservatism no longer lists Straussianism as a defining philosophy or prescriptive subtype. Some neoconservatives were influenced by Strauss or Straussians, but scholarship does not establish Straussian thought as the movement’s definition or foreign-policy program.
- Paleoconservatism no longer lists the Old Right and Traditionalism as child schools. They remain part of its lineage and intellectual context.
- One-Nation Conservatism no longer presents the broader descriptor “paternalistic conservatism” or the related historical label “Tory Democracy” as child ideologies.
- Fiscal Conservatism now carries only its thin fiscal philosophy. Supply-side economics and austerity are possible strategies, not necessary commitments or subtypes; generic conservative and classical-liberal values are no longer assigned to its normative or empirical layers.
- Social Conservatism no longer treats generic traditionalism labels as child schools or Communitarianism as an empirical-belief doctrine.
- National Conservatism no longer treats Realism as an inherent empirical doctrine and now carries a caution that contemporary movements vary in their relationship to liberal constraints.
- Liberal Conservatism no longer puts Classical Liberalism in the empirical layer. Both liberal-conservative hybrids now have explicit normative and practical summaries, while their descriptive layers remain unfilled rather than converting values into factual claims.

## Source basis

- “Conservatism,” _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_, on conservatism’s contested definition, living tradition, skepticism toward abstract redesign, and preference for gradual reform rather than simple reaction: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/conservatism/
- Michael Brenes and Michael Franczak, “Conservatives in a ‘Liberal Age’: Rethinking the Neoconservative Turn in the 1960s,” in _Cold War Liberalism_ (Cambridge University Press, 2026), on neoconservatism’s Cold War liberal roots and emphasis on national security and military power: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cold-war-liberalism/conservatives-in-a-liberal-age/BC44D4337E77BD075DC6247719788839
- Michael Zuckert, “Straussians,” in _The Cambridge Companion to Leo Strauss_ (Cambridge University Press, 2009), on the uncertain label and the lack of an established connection between Strauss’s thought and the political actions attributed to neoconservatives: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-leo-strauss/straussians/66102B19A2278D5FE80BEF4B265CFBA3
- Rita Abrahamsen et al., “A Diverse and Global Right,” in _World of the Right_ (Cambridge University Press, 2024), on the 1980s coinage of paleoconservatism, its Old Right revival, and its opposition to neoconservative and neoliberal currents: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/world-of-the-right/diverse-and-global-right/14C1EDB3F6A184F7210761521EDF9919
- Peter Kolozi, “The Paleoconservative Critique of Global Capitalism,” in _Conservatives Against Capitalism_ (Columbia University Press, 2017), on paleoconservative economic nationalism, opposition to free trade, and concern with national culture and immigration: https://academic.oup.com/columbia-scholarship-online/book/16897/chapter/174138181
- Robert M. Page, “Towards a One Nation Conservative welfare state?,” in _Clear Blue Water?_ (Policy Press, 2015), on the post-1950 One Nation Group’s cost-effective, non-egalitarian welfare state: https://academic.oup.com/policy-press-scholarship-online/book/19415/chapter-abstract/178030271
- Julian E. Zelizer, _Taxing America: Wilbur D. Mills, Congress, and the State, 1945–1975_ (Cambridge University Press, 1999), on the historical variability of fiscal conservatism and its compatibility with tax reductions, occasional deficits, spending limits, and contributory social insurance: https://assets.cambridge.org/97805216/21663/sample/9780521621663ws.pdf
- Pew Research Center, “The Political Typology” (2011), on the empirical separability of economic and social conservatism and the existence of socially conservative groups with different views of government programs: https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2011/05/04/section-1-the-political-typology-2/
- Görkem Altınörs and Angelos Chryssogelos, “Beyond populism and into the state: The political economy of national-conservatism,” _British Journal of Politics and International Relations_ 26, no. 4 (2024), on national conservatism’s traditional hierarchy, national sovereignty, state economic role, and variable break with transnational neoliberalism: https://doi.org/10.1177/13691481241246538
- Kenneth Dyson, _Conservative Liberalism, Ordo-Liberalism, and the State_ (Oxford University Press, 2021), on conservative liberalism’s cross-national variety, historical contingency, overlapping family resemblances, and lack of one finalized form: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780192596208_A41112075/preview-9780192596208_A41112075.pdf

The catalog-specific distinction between Conservative Liberalism and Liberal Conservatism is an editorial convention for keeping two result profiles legible. It is an inference from their current family placement and centroids, not a claim that the names have one settled cross-national meaning.
