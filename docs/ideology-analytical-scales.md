# Analytical scale guidance for ideology labels

The public taxonomy distinguishes a label’s role (primary, modifier, specialist,
context, or retired) from the scale at which a researcher may study the label.
Scale is a unit-of-analysis aid, not a sixth taxonomy role and not a claim that
respondents belong to a social group.

## Working vocabulary

- **Macro**: doctrine, canon, social order, population, or society-wide institutional pattern.
- **Meso**: party, movement, organization, coalition, regime project, or public political discourse.
- **Micro**: individual uptake, local practice, household, or interactional use. The relevant unit is set by the question; “micro” is not automatically the individual in every analysis.
- **Nano**: a proposed, method-sensitive sublevel within micro for personally adapted specific ideas, dispositions, and interactional mechanisms. It is not a standalone ideology category here and is not inferred from a single response.

The registry therefore gives broad primary traditions the common scales `macro`
and `meso`. Specialist traditions, cross-cutting modifiers, and institutional
or strategic context entries receive `meso` and `macro` guidance. This reflects
their usual use as movements, political discourse, organizations, institutional
projects, and broader doctrines. Active primary and modifier entries also
record `micro` as the respondent-measurement scale: the estimate concerns
individual uptake of those broader claims, not a new micro ideology. Provisional
specialists remain at the catalog scale until an opt-in module result is shown;
context entries are not respondent-measured.

The micro level remains important for interpreting a respondent’s answers: an
individual can adopt, reject, reinterpret, or combine parts of a tradition.
The nano proposal goes further inside that micro level, toward personally
adapted ideas and dispositions that may not be consciously articulated. It
requires methods suited to that question and should not be inferred from one
answer or reported as a separate “nano ideology.” Likewise, a single
interaction cannot be inferred to express a complete ideology.

## Evidence and limits

Humphrey, Laycock, and Umbach’s introduction to a *Journal of Political
Ideologies* special issue distinguishes related macro, meso, and micro routes
through which ideological discourse is canonically defined, politically used,
and taken up in everyday concepts:
<https://doi.org/10.1080/13569317.2019.1589961>.

Ylikoski’s *Synthese* discussion cautions that micro, meso, and macro are
context-dependent and that the same entity can occupy different scales in
different explanatory settings:
<https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-024-04841-3>.

Leader Maynard’s chapter in the *Routledge Handbook of Ideology Analysis*
proposes a four-level matrix—macro environment, meso group, micro individual,
and nano sub-individual—and distinguishes this analytic vocabulary from the
different macro/meso/micro purpose used by Humphrey, Laycock, and Umbach:
<https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003412007-3>.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s entry on ideology supplies the
broader social-content and social-effects baseline, but does not prescribe a
fixed scale hierarchy for this catalog:
<https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ideology/>.

The implementation is recorded as `2026-08-analytical-scale-v2` and is exposed
on catalog cards so users can see the scale boundary alongside the role and
measurement status.
