# Anticolonial, Indigenous, and separatist layer guide

Date: 2026-08-11

## Scope

This focused editorial pass adds the missing descriptive layer summaries for
`indigenism`, `left-wing-nationalism`, and `separatist-nationalism`. Their
direct term definitions and normative or practical summaries were already
specific, but their descriptive layers fell back to broad family language or
did not distinguish the relevant institutional expectations. The new copy
keeps Indigenous sovereignty, anticolonial nationalism, and separatist
self-government distinct. No centroids, axis weights, question prompts, or
scoring behavior changed.

## Layer decisions

- **Indigenous Sovereignty / Self-Determination** now describes the expectation
  that imposed state or market institutions have disrupted Indigenous
  authority, land relationships, cultural continuity, and self-government. It
  preserves the diversity of Indigenous traditions over sovereignty,
  territory, recognition, and relations with non-Indigenous institutions.
- **Left-Wing Nationalism** now describes the expectation that imperial
  domination and unequal international integration obstruct social equality
  and self-government, while national liberation and popular sovereignty can
  open conditions for redistribution or anticolonial transformation. It also
  records tensions with internationalism and minority claims.
- **Separatist Nationalism** now describes the expectation that a distinct
  regional or national community can gain self-government through autonomy,
  federal reorganization, or independence. Comparative research is reflected
  in the qualification that autonomy’s relationship to secession is
  conditional, not mechanically positive or negative.

These are descriptive accounts of characteristic arguments and observed
institutional debates, not endorsements of any specific territorial claim or
prediction that one constitutional outcome is universally correct. The
separatist label does not establish a unilateral right to secession or a
violent strategy.

## Source basis

- Adom Getachew, “A Political Theory of Decolonization,” in *Worldmaking after
  Empire* (Princeton University Press, 2019), on anticolonial nationalism,
  empire, unequal international integration, and projects of political
  worldmaking:
  https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691179155.003.0002
- Sarah A. B. Prout and others, “Indigenous Sovereignty and Political Science:
  Building an Indigenous Politics Subfield,” *Annual Review of Political
  Science* 27 (2024), on Indigenous understandings of settler colonialism,
  sovereignty, authority, and the limits of disciplinary exclusion:
  https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-041322-050512
- Heidi Kiiwetinepinesiik Stark, “The Two Row Wampum: Decolonizing and
  Indigenizing Democratic Autonomy,” *Polity* 54, no. 2 (2022), on relational
  Indigenous autonomy and plural political arrangements:
  https://doi.org/10.1086/718331
- André Lecours, “Nationalism and Secessionism: Autonomy, Static and Dynamic,”
  in *Nationalism, Secessionism, and Autonomy* (Oxford University Press, 2021),
  on how changing forms of autonomy can shape secessionist pressure:
  https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780192846754.003.0002
- David S. Siroky and John Cuffe, “Lost Autonomy, Nationalism and Separatism,”
  *Comparative Political Studies* 48, no. 1 (2015), on the conditional effects
  of current, absent, and lost autonomous arrangements:
  https://doi.org/10.1177/0010414013516927
