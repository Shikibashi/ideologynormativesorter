# Liberal and cosmopolitan term-guide seventeenth pass

Date: 2026-08-11

## Scope

This pass reviewed eight result names that still fell back to generic liberal,
cosmopolitan, or world-federalist patterns: Market Liberal, Decentralist
Market Liberal, Civil-Libertarian Cosmopolitan, Classical Liberalism,
Market-Governance Liberalism / Neoliberalism, Social Liberalism, World
Federalism, and Multiculturalism. It adds direct guides, layer summaries, and
regression tests. It does not change centroids, axis weights, question
prompts, question selection, result eligibility, or scoring behavior.

## Direct term guides

- **Market Liberal** now identifies the market-oriented liberal combination of
  private property, individual rights, competitive exchange, rule of law, and
  limited but constitutional government. It is separated from social
  liberalism’s stronger emphasis on public capability provision and from the
  catalog’s narrower neoliberal or market-governance label.
- **Decentralist Market Liberal** now identifies a market-liberal position that
  treats concentrated state power as a central danger and favors decentralized
  exchange, voluntary association, and exit. It is not used as a synonym for
  socialist anarchism or every anti-state position.
- **Civil-Libertarian Cosmopolitanism** now combines civil-libertarian
  skepticism of concentrated authority with obligations beyond national
  borders, while leaving property, markets, and global institutional design
  open.
- **Classical Liberalism** now identifies the broad tradition centered on
  individual liberty, private property, freedom of contract, rule of law, and
  constitutional limits. Historical versions vary over welfare, democracy,
  and the state’s economic role; the term is not collapsed into contemporary
  libertarianism.
- **Market-Governance Liberalism / Neoliberalism** now uses the catalog’s
  narrower sense: governing capitalism through competition, privatization or
  outsourcing, expert institutions, and international rules. The broader
  word “neoliberalism” remains historically contested and often polemical.
- **Social Liberalism** now identifies the liberal synthesis of individual
  rights and equal citizenship with public action for capabilities,
  opportunity, and protection from severe deprivation. It is not treated as
  socialism or as one fixed welfare-state design.
- **World Federalism** now identifies a democratic federal layer of global
  government with shared authority between global institutions and nations.
  It is stronger than international cooperation alone without requiring the
  abolition of national governments or one economic system.
- **Multiculturalism** now identifies normative support for recognition,
  accommodation, or group-differentiated rights for distinct communities
  within a shared political order. It is distinguished from the demographic
  fact of diversity, forced assimilation, separatism, and any single model of
  integration.

Each guide is keyed to the stable catalog id and shown alone, so a specific
result is not displaced by a generic parent-family definition.

## Layer summaries

All eight labels now have explicit normative, descriptive, and prescriptive
summaries. The summaries keep neighboring liberal terms from becoming one
undifferentiated preference:

- Market Liberal and Classical Liberalism emphasize property, exchange,
  liberty, and constitutional limits, while Social Liberalism adds a stronger
  public responsibility for capabilities and protection from deprivation.
- Market-Governance Liberalism / Neoliberalism describes a particular way of
  governing capitalism through competition, expert institutions, and
  international rules; the entry records the term’s contested usage.
- The cosmopolitan entries distinguish civil-libertarian universal obligations
  from World Federalism’s specific federal institutional proposal.
- Multiculturalism is treated as a normative response to cultural diversity,
  not diversity itself or a demand for separate sovereign communities.

The Decentralist Market Liberal result is a catalog-specific compound synthesis
of its displayed market, voluntary-association, and exit commitments rather
than a claim that scholarship uses one universally standardized label for it.
These are editorial descriptions of the catalog profiles, not empirical claims
about every adherent or movement using any of the names.

## Source basis

- “Liberalism,” _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_, on liberalism as a
  diverse tradition, the contrast between classical and new liberal positions,
  private property and markets, and disagreement over the state’s role:
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liberalism/
- “Neoliberalism,” _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_, on the contested
  meaning of the term and its association with liberal-capitalist institutions,
  limited democracy, modest welfare, market coordination, and skepticism of
  extensive regulation:
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/neoliberalism/
- “Multiculturalism,” _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (substantive
  revision 2024), on the distinction between demographic diversity and
  multiculturalism as a normative ideal, rejection of forced assimilation, and
  debates over group-differentiated rights:
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/multiculturalism/
- “Cosmopolitanism,” _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_, on versions that
  focus on moral obligations, global justice, shared markets, culture, or
  political institutions rather than one single world-state model:
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cosmopolitanism/
- Young World Federalists, “World Federalism,” a movement account of adding a
  democratic global layer while retaining national sovereignty for national
  issues:
  https://www.ywf.world/worldfederalism
- World Government for the Federation of Earth, an example of a primary
  world-federalist proposal describing a non-military, democratic, federal
  structure with ultimate sovereignty in the people:
  https://www.ef-gov.org/en/index.php
- Christian Joppke, “Liberalism versus Neoliberalism,” _Political
  Neoliberalism: Order and Rupture_ (Oxford University Press, 2025), on why
  liberalism and neoliberalism should not be treated as interchangeable
  political doctrines:
  https://academic.oup.com/book/59990/chapter-abstract/513445521
- The catalog does not rely on a single canonical source for the compound
  “Decentralist Market Liberal.” Its definition is an explicit editorial
  synthesis of the label’s existing metadata, with the catalog’s caution note
  preserving its distinction from socialist anarchism and left-wing market
  anarchism.

The catalog’s decisions about which related traditions are required
philosophies, influences, or strategies are editorial taxonomy choices
informed by these sources. They are not claims that scholarship uses one
universally settled classification for every label.
