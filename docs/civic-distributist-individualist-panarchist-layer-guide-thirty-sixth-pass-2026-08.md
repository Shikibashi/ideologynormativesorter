# Civic, distributist, individualist-anarchist, and panarchist layer guide

Date: 2026-08-11

## Scope

This focused editorial pass adds the missing descriptive layer summaries for
`civic-nationalist`, `distributism`, `individualist-anarchism`, and
`panarchism`. Their direct term definitions and normative or practical
summaries were already specific, but their descriptive layers either fell
back to broad family language or had no curated account. The new copy keeps
four different claims separate: national solidarity through civic membership,
economic independence through dispersed ownership, autonomy through voluntary
cooperation, and nonterritorial political choice. No centroids, axis weights,
question prompts, or scoring behavior changed.

## Layer decisions

- **Civic Nationalism** now describes the expectation that shared citizenship,
  political institutions, and public principles can build solidarity across
  ancestry or religion. It also records the scholarly caution that the civic
  and ethnic models are not cleanly separable and that civic criteria can be
  exclusionary in practice.
- **Distributism** now describes the expectation that concentrated ownership
  weakens economic independence and political freedom, while dispersed
  productive property and local associations can support autonomy and human
  dignity. The wording identifies this as a political-economic research
  program rather than a settled empirical result.
- **Individualist Anarchism** now describes the expectation that compulsory
  authority and group claims can threaten individual autonomy, while leaving
  open whether voluntary cooperation produces market, mutualist, or sharing
  arrangements. This preserves the historical family’s internal diversity.
- **Panarchism** now describes the expectation that territorial monopoly is not
  required for legitimate government, with voluntary nonterritorial states and
  exit allowing multiple governance arrangements. Feasibility, coordination,
  and unequal bargaining power remain theoretical questions.

These are descriptive accounts of characteristic arguments, not endorsements
or claims that any of these proposed mechanisms has been established as a
general social-science law.

## Source basis

- Andrea Simonsen and Bart Bonikowski, “Is Civic Nationalism Necessarily
  Inclusive? Conceptions of Nationhood and Anti-Muslim Attitudes in Europe,”
  *European Journal of Political Research* (2019), on the blurred boundary
  between civic and ethnic nationalism and the possibility that civic ideals
  can legitimize exclusion:
  https://www.bu.edu/european/files/2019/09/10.16.19Bonikowski.pdf
- Tamar H. Meisels, “Not So Civic: Is There a Difference between Ethnic and
  Civic Nationalism?,” *Annual Review of Political Science* 22 (2019), on the
  theoretical distinction and its blurred empirical boundaries:
  https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-022018-024059
- Alexander William Salter, *The Political Economy of Distributism: Property,
  Liberty, and the Common Good* (2023), on dispersed property ownership,
  economic independence, Catholic social teaching, and the status of
  distributism as a research program and reform blueprint:
  https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.3485520
- “Anarchism,” *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, on individualist
  anarchism’s focus on autonomy, voluntary cooperation, and disagreement over
  market or communal outcomes:
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/
- Susan Love Brown, “Individualist Anarchism,” *International Encyclopedia of
  Revolution and Protest* (2010), on the historical formation and diversity of
  individualist anarchism:
  https://doi.org/10.1002/9781405198073.wbierp1699
- Davide Saracino, “The Birth of Panarchism: Voluntary Non-Territorial States
  in the Works of Gustave De Molinari and Paul Émile De Puydt,” *European
  Journal of Political Theory* (2025), on voluntary nonterritorial states,
  explicit contracts, and the theory’s political-legitimacy problem:
  https://doi.org/10.1177/14748851251327155
